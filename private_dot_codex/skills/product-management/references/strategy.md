# Product strategy

Use this reference to choose where to play, how to win, and what not to pursue.

Strategy is a coherent set of choices under constraints, not a collection of desirable outcomes.

## Frame the strategic decision

Capture the product stage, time horizon, business model, triggering change, major constraints, and the decision that must be made now.
Separate durable choices from hypotheses that still need validation.

## Strategy canvas

Cover only the sections needed, or use all nine for a full strategy:

1. Vision: the durable change the product seeks to create.
2. Target segments: prioritized users or customers, plus explicitly excluded segments.
3. Pain and value: costly problems and the value of solving them.
4. Value proposition: why the chosen segment should switch from its current alternative.
5. Trade-offs: what the product will not optimize or build, and why.
6. Metrics: outcome, input, and guardrail measures.
7. Growth engine: the mechanism by which adoption compounds.
8. Core capabilities: capabilities that must be built, bought, or partnered for.
9. Defensibility: evidence-backed sources of advantage, or an honest statement that none exists yet.

For each section, distinguish committed choice, supported inference, and hypothesis.

## Vision and value proposition

A useful vision is directional, durable, and specific enough to constrain choices.
Avoid market-leader clichés and arbitrary dates.

Build the value proposition from:

- who is in the target situation;
- why the current situation is costly;
- what they do before the product;
- how the product changes the job;
- what outcome follows;
- why alternatives are less suitable.

## Business-model choices

Choose the canvas that matches the decision:

- Lean Canvas: early venture hypotheses and riskiest unknowns.
- Business Model Canvas: operating model and partner/resource structure.
- Strategy Canvas: competitive choices, trade-offs, and defensibility.
- Combined startup view: only when the user needs strategy and economics together.

Do not fill every box with generic text.
Highlight contradictions between value creation, delivery cost, channel, and revenue.

## Market scan

Use a framework only when it answers a live question:

- SWOT: synthesize internal strengths/weaknesses and external opportunities/threats into actions.
- PESTLE: scan current macro forces with dated sources.
- Porter's Five Forces: examine industry economics and bargaining power.
- Ansoff Matrix: compare growth risk across existing/new products and markets.

Avoid running all four by default.
When current market claims are involved, browse and cite sources.

## Monetization and pricing

Start with value metric, buyer, user, purchase process, alternatives, willingness-to-pay evidence, marginal cost, and strategic goal.
Compare a small number of viable models.

For each option, show:

- who pays and for what unit of value;
- packaging and price fences;
- adoption and expansion mechanism;
- revenue and cost implications;
- abuse, cannibalization, and support risks;
- validation experiment and threshold.

Treat competitor prices as context, not proof of willingness to pay.

## Red-team the strategy

Identify the claims that carry the most weight.
For each, provide the strongest counterargument, failure mechanism, leading indicator, cheapest discriminating test, and kill or revisit criterion.
Do not manufacture balance when evidence clearly favors one direction.

## Strategy result

Lead with:

1. recommended choice and why now;
2. target segment and excluded alternatives;
3. value proposition and strategic trade-offs;
4. capabilities, growth mechanism, and economics;
5. metrics and guardrails;
6. load-bearing assumptions and tests;
7. what would change the recommendation.
