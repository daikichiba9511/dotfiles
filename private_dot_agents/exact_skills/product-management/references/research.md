# Product research

Use this reference to turn user, market, and competitor evidence into decision-relevant findings.

## Start with the decision and corpus

Define:

- the decision the research should inform;
- included and excluded sources;
- collection period and sample size;
- relevant user or market population;
- known sampling, survivorship, and channel biases.

Build a source inventory before synthesizing.
Do not treat a product description as research data.

## Confidence model

Classify findings by support:

- High: repeated across independent behavioral or direct evidence sources.
- Medium: consistent but limited, indirect, or concentrated in one channel.
- Low: plausible interpretation based on sparse or biased evidence.
- Hypothesis: useful proposition with no direct supporting evidence yet.

Preserve disconfirming and minority evidence instead of averaging it away.

## User research synthesis

### Jobs and needs

Extract the situation, motivation, desired outcome, current alternative, friction, and consequence.
Prefer concrete episodes over general opinions.

### Segmentation

Segment by behavior, need, context, constraint, or willingness to change.
Use demographics only when they explain a decision-relevant difference.

For each segment, state:

- defining rule;
- observed evidence and sample size;
- primary job and pain;
- current alternative;
- product fit and likely value;
- uncertainty and counterexamples.

### Personas

Create personas only when they compress meaningful, recurring patterns that alter product choices.
If data is insufficient, call them `proto-personas` and list the tests needed to validate them.
Avoid fictional names, portraits, or decorative biographies unless they improve the user's artifact.

### Journey mapping

Select stages that match the actual product rather than forcing a standard funnel.
For each stage, map trigger, actor, action, touchpoint, expected outcome, emotion if evidenced, friction, workaround, and opportunity.
Identify handoffs and drop-offs where evidence supports them.

### Feedback and sentiment

Normalize duplicates and distinguish volume from severity.
Group feedback by job, problem, segment, and lifecycle stage.
Sentiment is a weak signal on its own; connect it to concrete reasons and behavior.
Report contradictory themes and the share of uncategorized evidence.

## Competitor research

Use current primary sources such as product pages, documentation, pricing pages, release notes, and public filings where applicable.
Record access dates.

Distinguish:

- direct competitors solving the same job for the same segment;
- indirect competitors solving the job differently;
- substitutes and the status quo, including manual work.

Compare decision-relevant dimensions rather than producing an indiscriminate feature grid.
Separate verified facts from inferences about strategy or performance.

## Market sizing

Use both approaches when the evidence permits:

- Top-down: begin with a credible population or spend estimate, then narrow transparently.
- Bottom-up: number of reachable accounts or users × realistic annual value.

Define TAM, SAM, and SOM for this specific product and route to market.
Show formulas, units, dates, sources, and sensitivity ranges.
Never present a single precise number when the inputs are uncertain.

## Research result

Lead with the findings that change a decision:

1. decision and recommendation;
2. corpus and important limitations;
3. findings with evidence and confidence;
4. segments, journeys, or competitor comparisons only where useful;
5. implications for product, strategy, or go-to-market;
6. contradictions and open questions;
7. next research ranked by information value.
